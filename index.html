How it works

  1. Read. The file is parsed in your browser — headers, frame rate, tracks.
  2. Plan. Shrinkray picks a bitrate that lands under your target, dropping resolution or frame rate only when it has to.
  3. Encode. Your browser's WebCodecs encoder (usually the GPU) writes a new MP4. If it overshoots, it tightens up and goes again.

Your video stays yours

No upload, no account, no watermark, no size limit, no queue behind someone else's 4K wedding video. The only network request after the page loads is one anonymous counter tick when a compression finishes — a single number, so the tally above can be honest. No file names, sizes, or contents are sent anywhere.

It's open source. Read the code, or run it from your own computer.

Fine print

Why 18 MB for email, not 25?

Gmail, Outlook and iCloud advertise 25 MB, but attachments are base64-encoded in transit, which adds about a third. A 25 MB video becomes a 33 MB message and bounces. 18 MB lands safely.

Why did it lower the resolution?

Because at your target size there weren't enough bits per pixel to keep the frame clean. Smaller-but-sharp beats big-and-blocky. Force a resolution in Options if you disagree.

It came out a bit under the target. Why not exactly?

Hardware encoders don't hit a bitrate precisely, so Shrinkray aims 7% low and re-encodes if it overshoots. Under is the point.

Which browsers?

Chrome, Edge, Brave, Arc, Opera and Safari 17+ on desktop and phone; Firefox 130+. Older Firefox and some Linux builds can't encode H.264 — the page will tell you.

Something broke.

Open an issue with your browser and the file's format. Real reports fix real bugs.
